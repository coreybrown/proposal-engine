# Competition: Next on Wembley

## Bottom line
- **KILL:** Free or near-free substitutes already do the core job — decide what a couple watches together — with far less setup than this product requires, and the closest scale analog to a logging-heavy tracker (TV Time, 20M+ registered users) shut down in 2026 because, in its own operator's words, "it was no longer sustainable to continue operating the service as a free app, and there was not enough demand for a paid app" (TechCrunch, fetched 2026-09-25). The "for couples" swipe-match niche (Matched, MatchWatch, Shared Watchlist: Couple Match, WeWatch Together, MatchaFilm) is already crowded, occupying exactly the low-effort positioning this product would need to win, while showing weak or negligible traction of its own. Trakt already holds both partners' watch history and a friend graph, and its own users have an unresolved, years-old request for a two-person overlap comparison — the fastest, cheapest path to this feature belongs to an incumbent, not a new entrant.
- **Your questions:** Q1: existing comparable trackers charge $0–$5/month typically (Simkl Supporter $1/mo, Trakt VIP ~$2.50–5/mo equivalent, JustWatch Pro $2.49/mo), and the couple-specific apps found are mostly free or low-traction paid, so no clean evidence anyone pays meaningfully for this exact job. Q2: every live couple-matching app sidesteps the onboarding problem by not requiring imported watch history at all (live swipe sessions or a light want-to-watch list); this is the one clear workaround, but it means abandoning the deep personalization the idea is built around. Q3: the closest low-frequency, logging-heavy analog (TV Time) proved retention/monetization does not survive at that frequency even at 20M+ users; low-cost tracker add-ons ($1–3/mo) or one-time purchases (TV Time's $74.99 lifetime tier) are the only pricing patterns with any signal of tolerance. Q5: the observed price band across comparable trackers and couple apps is roughly $0–$40/year per person (see KEY_FIGURES), well below what a TMDb-licensed, LLM-driven product would likely need to charge to cover its cost to serve.
- **Assumptions:** A5 contradicted — free, purpose-built two-person overlap tools already exist (Matched, MatchWatch, WeWatch Together) and the harder, deeper version of the same idea (persistent watch history + LLM ranking) is a plausible near-term feature add for Trakt or Simkl, who already hold the data and have an open user request for it. A2 partly addressed — workarounds exist industry-wide, but every one of them abandons history-based personalization rather than solving the import problem. A3 evidenced negatively by TV Time's operator-stated shutdown reason.

## The graveyard

- **TV Time (Whip Media), shut down July 15, 2026.** The single most relevant data point in this file. It was a manual/lightly-automated per-person TV episode tracker with 20M+ registered users at its peak (Whip Media press release, cited via search 2026-09-25) and social/notification features close in shape to the "logging plus discovery" half of this idea. Whip Media's own statement: "it was no longer sustainable to continue operating the service as a free app, and there was not enough demand for a paid app" (TechCrunch, fetched 2026-09-25: https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/02/popular-tv-tracking-app-tv-time-is-shutting-down-as-company-focuses-on-ai/). Coverage also notes the company is redirecting effort toward enterprise AI products instead (same source). This directly tests A3 and A8: a product with a very similar behavioural ask (manual per-person logging, used between viewing sessions) could not sustain a paid version even at a scale two to three orders of magnitude larger than anything this idea has reached.
- **The broader "Tinder for movies/TV" category.** A secondary source (MatchWatch's own blog, which is itself a competitor, so read with that bias in mind) describes the category as one that "has continued breeding new entrants" since roughly 2018, with the implication that most go quiet within about 18 months (matchwatch.tv/blog, cited via search 2026-09-25). This is a low-confidence, self-interested source, but it is consistent with the directly observed evidence: two of the five direct competitors found (Shared Watchlist: Couple Match, WeWatch Together) have 1–2 App Store ratings each despite being live, listed apps — a sign of a crowded field with mostly unproven entrants rather than one or two winners.
- **Trakt's 2025 price hike and defections.** Not a shutdown, but a graveyard-adjacent signal: when Trakt doubled its VIP price and removed grandfathered rates, it triggered visible user backlash and named defections to Simkl, Letterboxd and Showly (AlternativeTo, fetched 2026-09-25). This shows the exact customer base this idea would draw from (people who already track TV) is price-sensitive and mobile between near-substitutable trackers, which caps pricing power for any new entrant in the same space.

## Positioning map

**Axes chosen:** the two dimensions map directly onto the two riskiest untested assumptions in the frame (A2 and A5), which is also what S1/S2 couples say they care about in the routed questions — how much work the tool demands before it's useful, and whether it actually solves the two-person problem or just the one-person problem.

- **X — Manual setup/data burden** (0 = works instantly with no account or history; 10 = both partners must enter full watch history, ratings, in-progress shows and subscriptions before it's useful).
- **Y — Purpose-built for two-person overlap** (0 = single-viewer tool, no couple feature; 10 = the entire product is organized around a shared decision between two people).

Lines:
- Next on Wembley: x=9, y=9, because it requires both partners' full watch history, ratings, in-progress shows and subscriptions before recommendations are useful, and the whole product is organized around a co-watch list plus two personal lists.
- Matched: x=2, y=8, because it needs only live swiping with no imported history, and is purpose-built for couple matching.
- MatchWatch: x=1, y=8, because it needs no account or history at all — a live shared swipe session for up to 6 people — and is explicitly a couple/group decision tool.
- Shared Watchlist: Couple Match: x=2, y=7, because it uses the same swipe-match mechanic aimed at couples, with negligible evidence of traction (1 rating).
- WeWatch Together: x=3, y=7, because partners hand-build a lighter want-to-watch list (not full history) and the app surfaces the overlap directly in a "For Us" tab.
- MatchaFilm: x=2, y=7, because it uses the same low-setup swipe-match mechanic for couples.
- Trakt: x=8, y=2, because accurate stats require ongoing manual logging or scrobbling, but the product is organized around one person's history; comparing with a partner is manual, and a real overlap-recommendation feature is an open, unresolved user request, not a shipped product.
- Simkl: x=7, y=2, because it requires similar per-person logging and has no couple-overlap product, only individual tracking with sync options.
- Serializd: x=6, y=1, because logging is manual and the product is an individual social diary with no couple feature.
- Letterboxd: x=6, y=1, because it's a manual, individual film diary; any couple feature or deep TV support is not core to the product.
- TV Time (defunct): x=6, y=2, because it required manual and partly-automatic per-person logging and had social/friends features, but no purpose-built couple-overlap recommender, and it shut down before a paid version proved sustainable.
- JustWatch: x=1, y=1, because it needs no setup and gives no personalized or couple-specific ranking, only availability search.
- Reelgood: x=2, y=1, because an optional light watchlist is the only setup, and there is no couple logic.
- Netflix Play Something / Household profiles: x=0, y=1, because the watch history behind the recommendation is already collected by the platform the household is already paying for, with zero extra setup — but each profile is recommended to individually, not as a pair.
- General-purpose AI assistant: x=1, y=4, because a user can describe both partners' tastes in a single prompt with no persistent setup, which weakly approximates an overlap, but it does not remember, rank, or check live availability across sessions without extra tooling.

**White space:** none identified at the point this product needs it. The low-x/high-y corner — purpose-built for couples, near-zero setup — is the most crowded part of the map, not empty: five live competitors (Matched, MatchWatch, Shared Watchlist, WeWatch Together, MatchaFilm) already sit there. The only open corner is high-x/high-y (heavy setup, couple-purpose-built), occupied solely by Next on Wembley. It is open because every other entrant in the "for couples" category has already chosen to minimize setup rather than maximize personalization depth — a revealed preference from five independent teams, not an overlooked opportunity.

## Wedge

The narrowest plausible wedge is **S2 (tracker-using couples) where at least one partner already has a real watch history in Trakt, Simkl or Letterboxd** — the entry cost of manual logging is already sunk for one partner, halving the setup problem for that pair specifically. An import layer against Trakt's public API (the most complete of the three) could let one partner join with their existing history and ask only the second partner to build theirs from scratch.

This wedge is narrow and short-lived, for two reasons found directly in this research:
1. It only reaches the sub-segment of S1 who already use a tracker — by definition a small slice of S1, and TV Time's shutdown shows even the full tracker category, at 20M+ users, could not fund itself on subscriptions.
2. Trakt's own users have an **open, unresolved, multi-year feature request** for exactly this kind of watched/ratings overlap comparison between two users (Trakt Forums, cited via search 2026-09-25), and Trakt already holds both users' history and a following graph. Trakt has also shipped comparison-adjacent features before (Year in Review, All-Time Stats, cited via search 2026-09-25) and has a paying VIP base that, per the 2025 price-hike episode, tolerates new paywalled features even amid public complaint. If this wedge showed any real traction, the harder half of the problem (getting people to log two people's history) is already solved inside Trakt or Simkl, and the remaining work — ranking the overlap and writing a "why" — is a much smaller build than a new entrant's full product.

No wedge was found that isn't already contested by an existing player with more data, more users, or lower switching costs.

## Moat

**None identified.** Specifically:
- **Network effects:** none beyond the pair itself; there is no cross-pair benefit, so growth doesn't get cheaper or the product better as more pairs join, unlike a social tracker with a following graph (which Trakt and Serializd already have).
- **Proprietary data:** none. TMDb and JustWatch-sourced provider data is available to any commercial licensee (per the frame's A6 finding), so no competitor is locked out of the same metadata.
- **Switching costs:** modest at best, and cut both ways. A manually built two-person history is mildly costly to abandon, which could help retention once a pair is in — but every comparable tracker already offers CSV or API export/import (Trakt has a public API; the frame confirms Netflix's own export is a manual CSV), so a rival can capture that same history without much friction.
- **Distribution:** none. The frame already establishes the builder has no audience or channel; nothing found in this research changes that.
- **License:** none, and possibly a liability — a TMDb commercial agreement is a cost and compliance burden every player in this space (Trakt, Simkl, JustWatch, Reelgood, this idea) must separately clear, not an advantage for any one of them.

## Incumbent response

- **Trakt or Simkl** are the most credible responders. Both already hold full watch history for any two users who choose to connect, already have a friend/following graph, have shipped comparison-adjacent features (Trakt's Year in Review, All-Time Stats), and Trakt already has an open, multi-year user request asking for exactly this overlap feature. Adding a ranked two-person overlap list with LLM-generated "whys" is an incremental feature on an existing data pipeline and paying user base, not a rebuild — plausibly shippable within one to two quarters if prioritized, materially faster than a solo builder without existing users or a data pipeline could reach comparable coverage.
- **Matched, MatchWatch, or a similar swipe-app player** could add a persistent "why" and a ranked list on top of their existing live-swipe session with an LLM prompt layer — also a light lift, since the couple UX and matching logic already exist; they would need to add persistence and personalization text, not the core mechanic.
- **Netflix** would need the least work of all to close the gap for its own subscribers specifically: it already has verified watch history for every profile in a household and already ships "Play Something"; extending that to a two-profile overlap view is plausible as a household feature, though nothing found in this research indicates Netflix has signaled intent to do so.

## What to steal, and what to avoid

**Steal:**
- The **zero-history swipe-session pattern** from Matched, MatchWatch and MatchaFilm removes the entire manual-onboarding problem (A2) by not requiring imported watch history at all — each session is live rather than dependent on prior logging.
- **MatchWatch's no-account model** lowers the second-partner activation barrier that A4 worries about; nothing to sign up for reduces the chance the second partner never completes setup.
- **TV Time's lifetime-purchase option** ($74.99, sourced above) is a hedge worth considering against the low-frequency-use churn risk in Q3: a one-time price removes the "cancel between uses" failure mode a monthly subscription can't survive.
- **Simkl's and JustWatch's $1–2.99/month tiers** show roughly what this demographic tolerates for a habitual, high-frequency tracker or guide — useful as a low anchor, with the caveat that this product is lower-frequency than either of those.

**Avoid:**
- **Trakt's mid-relationship price hike**, which stripped grandfathered pricing and triggered public backlash and named defections (AlternativeTo, fetched 2026-09-25) — a bad precedent for a product whose unit is a pair with jointly-invested, hand-entered data; breaking trust on price here risks losing both people in the account at once, not one.
- **TV Time's free-first, monetize-later sequencing.** Its own operator's statement — free wasn't sustainable to run, and paid didn't have demand, at 20M+ users — is a direct warning against assuming scale will solve the revenue question later, especially at this idea's tiny expected scale.
- **Matched's provider-accuracy complaint** (matched titles that turned out not to be on any of the user's five streaming services) is a specific, avoidable trust failure: if TMDb/JustWatch-sourced availability data goes stale or is wrong, users notice immediately and say so in reviews.

## Open questions for the decision-maker
1. Given that five live competitors already occupy the "purpose-built for couples, low setup" position this product would need to compete for, is the intended differentiator worth the extra manual-entry burden Q2 and A2 already flag as a risk — or should the product drop history-based personalization and start from a swipe-session model instead?
2. Given Trakt's open, unresolved user request for a two-person overlap comparison, is there a faster, lower-risk path to testing this idea as a feature pitch to an existing tracker (partnership, acquisition, or simply watching whether Trakt ships it) rather than a standalone product?
3. Does the TV Time shutdown change the revenue-model thinking in Q3/Q5 — specifically, would a one-time purchase (like TV Time's former lifetime tier) be worth testing ahead of a subscription, given the stated low-frequency use pattern?
