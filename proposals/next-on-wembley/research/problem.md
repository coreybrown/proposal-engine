# Problem validation: Next on Wembley

## Bottom line
- **Finding:** Outside the builder's household, real people complain about the "what should we watch" decision as a couple, and at least five independent teams have built apps to solve exactly that overlap problem — one of them (Matched) has ~170k downloads, a 4.7/5 rating and a paid tier. But every one of those comparable apps solves it with a swipe, not with the manual watch-history, ratings and in-progress logging this idea requires from both partners — and the closest cost analogue for that heavier ask, TV Time (25–26M users, free, manual logging), shut down in July 2026 because, in its own words, "there was not enough demand for a paid app."
- **Your questions:**
  - Q1: Yes, the problem exists beyond one household — organic complaints and five competing apps confirm it — but the only clean willingness-to-pay signal (Matched's $2.99/mo tier) belongs to a lighter-weight swipe product, not to one that asks for manual history entry.
  - Q2: Manual upkeep is tolerated by a minority of enthusiasts (Letterboxd: 30M+ users log manually; Trakt has paying VIP subscribers) but is explicitly named as the reason people abandon TV trackers, and the segment's own biggest free player (TV Time) could not convert scale into paid demand. Tolerance is real but partial, and it degrades the moment logging becomes a per-episode, per-partner chore rather than a one-time swipe session.
  - Q3: Frequency of the underlying "what to watch" decision looks high (a UserTesting survey puts individual decision time at ~110 hours/year), but no source measures how often a couple specifically starts a *new series* together. That figure is unknown, and it is the one this idea most needs.
- **Assumptions:** A1 holds outside the builder's household (organic complaints and five competing apps exist); A2 is contradicted for the heavier design this idea proposes (comparable apps use swiping, not full watch-history entry, and the segment's largest free tracker shut down over the paid version having too little demand).

## Evidence of the problem

### S1: Co-watching couples (general)
**Independent sources: 4** (App Store reviews — primary/direct; a blog-comment thread — direct; a third-party market-research survey; a company-run survey, weighted down for self-interest).

- **A validated micro-category already exists.** At least five apps target exactly this problem: *Matched: Movie App For Couples* (Taste Labs, Inc.), *WeWatch Together*, *Swatched*, *Shared Watchlist: Couple match*, and *Watch and Chill*. That five independent builders converged on the same wedge — swipe-based matching for couples — is itself a market signal, separate from any one app's traction.
- **Matched** (App Store, fetched 2026-09-25): 4.7/5 stars, 281 ratings. Reviews:
  - "This is the best movie picking assistant for couples who can't decide what to watch. Removes the endless, 'What do you wanna watch?'" — reviewer MuffinButt5002 (https://apps.apple.com/us/app/matched-movie-app-for-couples/id1623287922)
  - "We love this! My wife and I started using this to decide what to watch, and it's made it so much easier." — reviewer RevBobBlack (same link)
  - "My wife and I simply don't want movies or shows together... Now two days in we have a page of shows we have both marked as matches." — reviewer Mrdan27611 (same link)
  - Critical review: "You can't use the app by yourself... you can't even browse movies without someone else using it." — reviewer "Great Exercises!" (same link) — a complaint that confirms the two-person requirement is felt as friction, not just a feature.
  - AppBrain lists Matched (Android) at 100,000+ installs, with a separate estimate of ~170,000 across platforms (https://www.appbrain.com/app/matched-movie-app-for-couples/io.taste.matched, snippet retrieved via search 2026-09-25; the AppBrain page itself returned a 403 on direct fetch, so this figure is secondary and unverified against the primary page).
- **Direct comments from partners, not app reviewers:** on a Substack post asking for sci-fi-adjacent show recommendations for a mixed-taste couple (https://csteefel.substack.com/p/help-hubby-likes-sci-fi/comments, fetched 2026-09-25):
  - Donna McArthur: "The main reason my hubs and I end up not watching something is because of the effort it takes to find a program that is mutually agreeable."
  - Kim Van Bruggen: "Although we rarely watch together because we can't find shows we both like."
- **UserTesting / Talker Research survey** (n=4,000 across US/UK/Australia, fielded Nov 2–7, 2024; https://www.usertesting.com/resources/reports/stream-fatigue-goes-global, fetched 2026-09-25): "Americans spend an average of 110 hours per year, or nearly five full days, simply trying to decide what to watch." This measures individual/household decision fatigue broadly, not specifically two-person disagreement — the mismatch matters and is called out below.
- **Netflix's own 2017 global survey** (30,267 US respondents; reported by multiple outlets including techtimes.com and wctv.tv, original source is Netflix PR, not independently verified): 48% of partners admit to "streaming infidelity" (watching ahead alone), and 18% report it has caused verbal arguments. This is adjacent evidence of friction in shared viewing, not direct evidence of "can't agree on what to start next," and it is a vendor-commissioned survey, so it is weighted down accordingly.

### S2: Tracker-using couples
**Independent sources: 2** (a review-aggregator for an existing paid tracker; a company's own shutdown announcement, both direct/primary).

- **No organic evidence found of couples specifically pairing trackers together.** Searched: Serializd's own social/follow features (a "follow" mechanic exists; no dedicated couple-pairing feature found), Trakt's forums, and general search for "couple tracker" pairing behavior.
- **Manual logging is tolerated by a large enthusiast base, but it is a named cause of abandonment.** Letterboxd (film tracking, not TV, and single-player, not paired): "over 30 million members as of July 2026... users published more than 143 million reviews and logged nearly 900 million films in the past year" (multiple press aggregation, fetched 2026-09-25) — evidence manual logging can scale for enthusiasts on the film side.
- **Trakt.tv Trustpilot reviews** (1.5/5, 30 reviews, fetched 2026-09-25, https://www.trustpilot.com/review/trakt.tv): complaints center on a price increase ("from $30 to $60 per year"), feature paywalling, and UI regressions — not on the concept of tracking itself. The existence of paying VIP subscribers angry about a price hike is itself indirect evidence that some people already pay for TV tracking, but the sentiment is strongly negative (1.5/5).
- **TV Time shut down July 15, 2026.** It had 25–26 million registered members and was free. The company's own statement: "It was no longer sustainable to continue operating the service as a free app, and there was not enough demand for a paid app." (TechCrunch, https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/02/popular-tv-tracking-app-tv-time-is-shutting-down-as-company-focuses-on-ai/, fetched 2026-09-25; corroborated by techtimes.com). This is the single most load-bearing piece of evidence against A3/A8: a manual-logging TV companion app with two orders of magnitude more users than this idea will ever likely reach could not find a viable paid model.

## Solved or underserved
**Partially solved for the lighter-weight version of the problem; unsolved (and possibly unsolvable at this cost) for the heavier version this idea proposes.**

- The "help two people find overlap" problem already has multiple free or cheap, reasonably well-reviewed solutions (Matched: 4.7/5, 281 ratings). For couples willing to swipe through a preference list once, this problem looks solved well enough that most won't feel acute pain.
- What is *not* solved, and where this idea's differentiation actually sits, is a recommendation built from full watch history (not just swiped preference), in-progress state, and per-episode logging for both partners. No competitor found asks for that. That could be a wedge (a competitive-strategist call) — or it could be why this segment stays underserved: the market's revealed preference, from five apps converging on swiping, is that a lighter input is what people will actually do. TV Time's shutdown is a cautionary data point specifically about the heavier, logging-based model: it scaled to 25–26M free users doing exactly the kind of manual logging Next on Wembley requires, and still couldn't sustain a paid tier.

## Who has it worst
No segment shows both acute, frequent pain **and** an existing paid habit specific to the couple's-overlap problem. The closest fit is a hypothetical intersection of S1 and S2 — couples where at least one partner is already a Trakt or Letterboxd-style logger and pays for VIP/Pro features (Trakt has paying, if grumbling, VIP subscribers; Letterboxd has tens of millions of manual loggers and a paid Patron tier). That intersection has: (a) demonstrated willingness to pay for tracking-adjacent features, (b) demonstrated tolerance for manual logging, and (c) a partner who does not currently log, which is exactly the gap this idea's co-watch feature could fill. No source sizes this intersection or confirms it behaves as described — it is a candidate, not a finding, and the venture-strategist should treat it as such.

## Willingness-to-pay signals (observed behavior only)
- People pay $2.99/month or $19.99/year for Matched's premium tier, a lighter-weight (swipe-based) couple's overlap app (App Store, fetched 2026-09-25). What premium unlocks beyond the free matching feature is unclear from the fetched page — flagged as unknown, not assumed to be the core value prop.
- People pay Trakt's VIP fee (cited by reviewers as having risen "from $30 to $60 per year") for tracking features, though the same reviewers are angry about the price change (Trustpilot, fetched 2026-09-25, secondary quote — Trakt's own pricing page could not be fetched).
- People pay Letterboxd Patron (~$49/year per secondary sources) for enhanced manual-logging features, at massive scale (30M+ total users, unknown paid conversion rate — kind: unknown).
- **Negative signal, same weight class:** TV Time had 25–26 million free users doing manual logging and explicitly could not generate enough demand for a paid tier to stay open (TechCrunch, fetched 2026-09-25). No stated-intent quote should be read alongside this; it is the strongest behavioral data point in either direction, and it points against monetizing manual-logging-heavy TV companion products, even at a scale this idea has no plausible path to reaching.
- **No observed pricing or payment behavior specific to the two-person overlap + full-history-logging combination this idea proposes.** Nothing found is a clean analogue; every willingness-to-pay data point above is for either the lighter swipe version or the single-player logging version, never both together.

## What would validate this
1. **A 2-week concierge test with 10–15 non-builder couples** (drawn from S1/S2, e.g. recruited via a TV-tracker or "what to watch" community post): ask each pair to set up and maintain both profiles by hand for 2 weeks, and measure (a) how many complete both profiles, (b) how many are still logging at day 14, and (c) how often they actually needed a new pick in that window. This single test would settle Q2 (upkeep tolerance) and materially narrow Q3 (decision frequency) at near-zero cost.
2. **A message to the Matched, WeWatch Together, or Shared Watchlist review threads or support channels**, or a short interview with 5 of their reviewers, asking specifically whether they would want (and pay more for) a version built on real watch history instead of swiping. This tests the actual wedge (history-based vs. swipe-based) directly, rather than inferring it from adjacent products.

## Open questions for the decision-maker
1. Would you be willing to run the 10–15-couple concierge test above before committing further build time, given it directly tests the two riskiest assumptions (A1 tolerance, A2 upkeep) at low cost?
2. TV Time's 25–26M-user shutdown is the single most negative data point found. Does knowing that change your appetite for a manual-logging-based design, versus exploring a lighter, swipe-first onboarding that imports history later?
3. Matched's premium tier ($2.99/mo) is the only clean, comparable, couple-specific price point observed. Does that number feel like a viable price for a much heavier product, or does the added value (real history, LLM "whys") need to be tested against a higher price?
